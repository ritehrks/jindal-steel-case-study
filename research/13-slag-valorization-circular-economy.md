# Techno-Economic and Metallurgical Assessment of AOD and EAF Stainless Steel Slag Valorization

## 1. Introduction and Macro-Industrial Context
The global industrial economy is currently undergoing an unprecedented structural realignment driven by the imperative to achieve net-zero greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions by 2050. At the epicenter of this transition are the steel and cement sectors, which collectively account for approximately 15% to 17% of total anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions worldwide. Historically, integrated steel mills utilizing traditional Blast Furnace (BF) routes produced Granulated Blast Furnace Slag (GGBFS) as a by-product, a critical Supplementary Cementitious Material (SCM) in the construction market.

However, the rapid decarbonization of the steel industry via Electric Arc Furnace (EAF) and Direct Reduced Iron (DRI) technologies is disrupting this supply chain. This structural deficit necessitates the immediate, advanced valorization of secondary metallurgical by-products, specifically stainless steel slags generated from Electric Arc Furnaces (EAF) and Argon Oxygen Decarburization (AOD) vessels. This report provides an exhaustive techno-economic and metallurgical assessment of two primary valorization pathways: Mineral Carbonation (permanent CO2 sequestration) and advanced metallurgical conditioning to utilize processed slag as a next-generation SCM.

## 2. Metallurgical Genesis and Mineralogical Profiling of Stainless Steel Slag
The production of stainless steel is a duplex process, resulting in the generation of two distinct slag streams.

### 2.1 EAF Slag Chemistry and Phase Composition
The Electric Arc Furnace (EAF) serves as the primary smelting vessel. Chemically, EAF stainless steel slag is dominated by calcium oxide (CaO) and silicon dioxide (SiO2). It solidifies into a dense crystalline bulk, with reactive phases like merwinite/akermanite suites, specifically Ca3Mg(SiO4)2 (56.9%), and dicalcium silicate Ca2SiO4 (23%). Because of its coarse physical structure, it requires mechanical crushing to unlock hydraulic reactivity.

### 2.2 AOD Slag and the Secondary Refining Process
The Argon Oxygen Decarburization (AOD) process lowers carbon content without oxidizing chromium. AOD slag possesses a specific chemical signature (approx. 57.72% CaO, 31.69% SiO2) and is heavily dominated by dicalcium silicate (70-80%). 

### 2.3 The Dicalcium Silicate (β→γ) Polymorphic Transformation
The defining physical anomaly of AOD slag is its cooling behavior. As molten AOD slag cools, monoclinic β-C2S thermodynamically transitions into orthorhombic γ-C2S. This β→γ transition is accompanied by a massive 12% expansion in crystal lattice volume, causing the macroscopic slag to spontaneously auto-pulverize into microscopic dust—a phenomenon known as "dusting". While a historical hazard, this dusting is an extraordinary asset for mineral carbonation, generating massive specific surface area without energy-intensive mechanical grinding.

## 3. Pathway 1: Mineral Carbonation and Permanent Sequestration
Mineral carbonation emulates natural silicate weathering, reacting alkaline earth metals in slag with CO2 to form thermodynamically stable carbonates, providing true, permanent sequestration.

### 3.1 Reaction Thermodynamics and Kinetic Modeling
The carbonation of stainless steel slag is a multi-step reaction. The kinetic trajectory is described by the Unreacted Core Model (Shrinking Core Model). Initial rapid chemical reaction yields to internal mass-transfer (diffusion) control once an insoluble calcium carbonate shell precipitates around the unreacted core. 

### 3.2 Optimization of Process Parameters and the Temperature Paradox
Slurry-phase wet carbonation outperforms direct dry gas-solid carbonation. Optimal conditions for AOD slag require specific liquid-to-solid ratios, mechanical agitation, and a thermal threshold balancing Ca2+ extraction with aqueous CO2 saturation (typically 50°C to 80°C).

### 3.3 Heavy Metal Immobilization and Geochemical Stabilization
Carbonation provides geochemical stabilization. As free lime is consumed, the high initial pH of the slag (approx. 12.5) normalizes. This drop in basicity actively suppresses the thermodynamic solubility of heavy metal complexes, including toxic hexavalent chromium (Cr6+), preventing leaching and making it safe for civil engineering applications.

### 3.4 Commercial Scaling and Real-World Implementation
Technologies like Orbix's Carbstone (Belgium) process fine AOD slags into semi-dry pressed blocks cured in CO2 autoclaves without Portland cement. Carbon8 Systems (UK) deploys modular in-situ reactors at steel plants to carbonate EAF dust and slag, neutralizing hazardous waste into lightweight aggregate.

## 4. Pathway 2: Valorization as a Supplementary Cementitious Material (SCM)
Integrating slag as an SCM attacks the massive carbon intensity of the cement sector, where clinker production emits ~0.8-1.0 tonnes of CO2 per tonne.

### 4.1 Activating Stainless Steel Slag for Hydraulic Performance
Traditional air-cooled stainless steel slag has poor hydraulic reactivity. Metallurgists employ Rapid Quenching and Air Granulation (chilling molten slag rapidly to freeze the metastable β-phase or form reactive glass) or Carbonation-Cured Solidification (using the γ-C2S powder in belite binders that cure via CO2 exposure).

### 4.2 Overcoming Regulatory and Volumetric Stability Challenges
Volumetric instability caused by free lime and free magnesia must be mitigated via advanced metallurgical fluxing or controlled weathering (steam/micro-carbonation) to qualify the material for use in structural concrete (ASTM C989/EN 15167).

### 4.3 Commercial Watersheds in SCM Integration
- **Tata Steel IJmuiden and Ecocem MoU (Europe, 2026):** Partnership to utilize BOF/EAF slags as next-generation SCMs via Ecocem's ACT technology, cutting cement embodied carbon by up to 60%.
- **Jindal Stainless and Harsco Environmental (India, 2026):** A landmark $150M contract expansion to process AOD/EAF slags from Hisar and Jajpur for brickmaking and advanced SCM formulations.

## 5. The Dual Carbon Benefit: Scope 1 Mitigation and Scope 3 Avoidance
- **Scope 1 Emission Mitigation via Permanent Mineralization:** Mineralizing point-source captured CO2 into slag directly deducts from the facility's gross emission ledger, avoiding EU ETS carbon taxes.
- **Scope 3 (Category 11) Downstream Avoided Emissions:** Selling processed slag as SCM displaces highly carbon-intensive Portland cement clinker. This generates profound "Avoided Emissions" for the steelmaker, unlocking access to green capital and sustainability-linked bonds.

## 6. Techno-Economic Feasibility and Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)
- **CAPEX/OPEX:** Mineral carbonation slurry reactors require high CAPEX, but AOD "dusting" rescues economics by eliminating grinding OPEX. Granulation rigs for SCM demand high upfront thermal CAPEX but offer massive downstream market value.
- **LCA:** Systemic LCAs based on ISO 14040/14044 consistently demonstrate net-negative CO2 footprints for carbonated slag blocks and overwhelming systemic benefits for slag-substituted cement.

## 7. Strategic Outlook and Conclusions
The aggressive valorization of stainless steel slag unlocks an unparalleled dual carbon benefit. By permanently mineralizing captured emissions to reduce Scope 1 liabilities, while simultaneously supplying highly reactive clinker substitutes to generate Scope 3 avoided emissions, the steel industry structurally redefines its most problematic waste stream into a foundational commodity of the circular economy.
