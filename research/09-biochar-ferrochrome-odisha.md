# Feasibility and Techno-Economic Assessment of Biochar Substitution in High-Carbon Ferrochrome Production via Submerged Arc Furnaces: Focus on Odisha, India

## 1. Introduction to the Pyrometallurgical Decarbonization Imperative
The global metallurgical industry stands at a critical juncture, navigating the dual mandates of meeting escalating demand for fundamental alloys while urgently mitigating anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions. The steel industry and its associated ferroalloy supply chains are inherently carbon-intensive and energy-intensive. Globally, the steel sector is responsible for approximately 8% of total emissions, characterized by an average emission intensity of 1.89 tonnes of carbon dioxide per tonne of crude steel produced. In India, which currently ranks as the world's second-largest steel producer, the carbon footprint is significantly heavier. The Indian steel sector accounts for roughly 12% of the nation's total emissions, operating with an elevated emission intensity of approximately 2.5 tonnes of carbon dioxide per tonne of steel.

Within this broader industrial context, the production of High-Carbon Ferrochrome (HCFeCr)—an indispensable alloying agent for stainless steel that provides crucial corrosion resistance, hardness, and mechanical strength—presents a pronounced and highly specific decarbonization challenge. The conventional production of HCFeCr is executed almost exclusively in Submerged Arc Furnaces (SAFs), a pyrometallurgical process that relies heavily on fossil carbon. This fossil carbon, typically introduced as metallurgical coke, specialized low-ash coal, or semi-coke, serves a dual purpose: it acts as a chemical reductant to strip oxygen from the chromite ore, and it provides the necessary electrical resistance to generate the extreme Joule heating required for the smelting process. The reliance on these ancient, geologically sequestered carbonaceous materials results in significant Scope 1 emissions. Comprehensive life-cycle assessments and carbon footprint analyses indicate that ferrochrome production can emit anywhere from 3.3 to 10.3 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent per tonne of alloy, highly dependent on the regional electrical grid's carbon intensity and the specific reductant mix employed.

In response to these environmental imperatives and India's nationally determined contributions, the Ministry of Steel, Government of India, released a seminal regulatory and strategic framework in 2024 titled "Greening the Steel Sector in India: Roadmap and Action Plan". Developed from the extensive findings of 14 specialized task forces, this framework explicitly identifies the integration of biochar, or biocarbon, as a critical medium-term lever for process transition, sitting alongside energy efficiency, carbon capture, utilization, and storage, and green hydrogen integration. Biochar, a highly porous, carbon-rich substance derived from the thermochemical conversion of biomass in oxygen-limited environments, offers a uniquely viable drop-in solution to displace fossil reductants in SAFs without necessitating the complete redesign of existing metallurgical infrastructure.

The state of Odisha represents the undisputed epicenter of India's ferroalloy and stainless-steel manufacturing ecosystem. The region holds vast reserves of chromite ore, most notably located in the Sukinda Valley, and hosts massive, integrated industrial complexes operated by primary entities such as Indian Metals & Ferro Alloys Ltd, Tata Steel, Jindal Stainless, and Vedanta. Concurrently, Odisha possesses a rich agrarian and silvicultural base, generating substantial quantities of agricultural residues, such as paddy straw, alongside expansive natural and cultivated bamboo resources. This unparalleled confluence of massive metallurgical demand and abundant biogenic resources positions Odisha as the optimal geographic proving ground for validating the substitution of metallurgical coal and coke with sustainably sourced biochar.

This exhaustive report provides a granular techno-economic analysis of the feasibility, metallurgical implications, carbon abatement potential, and projected economic impact of replacing metallurgical carbon with biochar—specifically derived from bamboo and agricultural waste—in HCFeCr SAF operations within Odisha.

## 2. Pyrometallurgical Fundamentals of High-Carbon Ferrochrome Smelting
To rigorously evaluate the viability of biochar, it is essential to first deconstruct the highly sensitive pyrometallurgical mechanisms occurring within a Submerged Arc Furnace. The production of HCFeCr, which typically contains 65% to 70% chromium and 4% to 6% carbon, requires the carbothermic reduction of complex chromite ore, generalized by the spinel structure (Fe,Mg)O⋅(Cr,Al,Fe)2O3.

### 2.1 The Dual Role of Carbon in the SAF Ecosystem
In the standard SAF environment, the carbonaceous material fulfills two non-negotiable functions that govern the entire operation. First, it operates as the primary chemical reductant. As the burden descends through the furnace, the carbon reacts with the metal oxides embedded in the chromite ore to yield metallic iron and chromium, simultaneously evolving carbon monoxide gas. Second, it serves as an electrical heating element. The SAF relies on resistive, or Joule, heating. Alternating current is passed through massive carbon electrodes submerged deep into the burden. The carbonaceous bed provides the highly specific electrical resistivity required to generate intense thermal energy, sustaining the extreme temperature gradients between 1600°C and 1700°C necessary to melt the ore, drive the reduction kinetics, and maintain the fluidity of the resulting slag.

### 2.2 Thermodynamics of Carbothermic Reduction
The reduction of the chromite spinel matrix is a highly endothermic, stepwise process that unfolds across distinct thermal zones within the furnace. As the burden materials descend into the progressively hotter lower zones of the furnace, the more easily reducible iron oxides are stripped of oxygen first, followed by the highly stable chromium oxides. The primary global reactions orchestrating this transformation can be simplified conceptually:

Fe3O4 + 4C → 3Fe + 4CO
Cr2O3 + 3C → 2Cr + 3CO

Simultaneously, the extreme temperatures and carbon-rich environment at the electrode tips facilitate the formation of various carbides, which is the defining metallurgical characteristic of high-carbon ferrochrome:

3Cr2O3 + 13C → 2Cr3C2 + 9CO

Silica (SiO2) present in the ore gangue, ash, and externally added fluxes is also partially reduced under these conditions, leading to fractional silicon dissolving into the molten alloy. Concurrently, the unreduced refractory oxides, primarily alumina (Al2O3), magnesia (MgO), and calcia (CaO), report to the liquid slag phase. The equilibrium thermodynamics dictating these reactions—particularly the Boudouard reaction (C+CO2⇌2CO)—are extraordinarily sensitive to the physical reactivity and structural morphology of the carbon source used.

### 2.3 Energy Intensity and the Burden of Specific Consumption
The smelting of HCFeCr is among the most energy-intensive processes in heavy industry. Standard specific electrical energy consumption ranges widely from 3,190 kilowatt-hours per tonne to upward of 4,500 kilowatt-hours per tonne of alloy produced. This parameter depends heavily on the mineralogical grade of the ore, the extent of any pre-reduction prior to the SAF, the ratio of slag generated, and the electrochemical quality of the carbonaceous reducing agent. The substitution of conventional, highly engineered metallurgical coke with an alternative biogenic reductant like biochar inherently threatens to disrupt these finely balanced thermodynamic and electrical parameters. It is therefore critical to execute a precise physiochemical characterization of the proposed bio-reductants.

## 3. Physiochemical Profiling of Biochar Feedstocks
Biochar is synthesized via pyrolysis, a thermochemical process that involves the thermal degradation of biomass in the complete or near-complete absence of oxygen, at temperatures generally ranging from 300°C to 750°C. The resulting physiochemical properties of the biochar are entirely dictated by the lignocellulosic composition of the feedstock matrix and the precise parameters of the pyrolysis process, including the heating rate, residence time, and peak carbonization temperature. For large-scale metallurgical application in Odisha, two primary feedstocks present the greatest volume potential: bamboo and agricultural residues, predominantly paddy straw.

### 3.1 Bamboo Biochar: A High-Grade Carbon Source
Bamboo is a fast-growing, perennial, giant grass characterized by high structural density, rapid biomass generation, and significant carbon yields. Odisha features extensive natural and cultivated bamboo resources, rendering it a highly strategic feedstock.
When subjected to optimized pyrolysis, bamboo biochar exhibits properties that are exceptionally conducive to demanding metallurgical applications. Comprehensive material analyses of specific bamboo species, such as Dendrocalamus strictus, reveal an elemental carbon content approaching 89.66%, corresponding to calculated fixed carbon yields ranging from 81.85% to 85.16%. Crucially, bamboo biochar is characterized by remarkably low volatile matter, typically around 6.83%, and an exceptionally low inorganic ash content, generally between 2% and 5%. These parameters render it a near-ideal substitute for low-ash metallurgical coke.

Furthermore, during the outgassing of volatiles, bamboo biochar develops a highly organized porous architecture with a substantial specific surface area, reaching up to 123.6 square meters per gram, alongside a total pore volume ranging from 0.057 to 0.18 cubic centimeters per gram. While this extensive porosity is traditionally valued for environmental remediation, water filtration, and agricultural soil amendment due to its high cation exchange capacity, in the context of pyrometallurgy, it translates to exceptionally high reactivity with carbon dioxide. This elevated reactivity can dramatically accelerate the reduction kinetics in the upper, cooler zones of the SAF.

### 3.2 Agricultural Residue Biochar: Paddy Straw and the Ash Challenge
India generates over 135 million tonnes of rice annually, with a vast proportion concentrated in eastern agrarian states like Odisha. Approximately 500 pounds of rice husks and straw are generated for every tonne of rice produced. Currently, up to 80% of this paddy straw is disposed of via catastrophic open-field burning, causing severe atmospheric pollution, contributing heavily to regional black carbon emissions, and devastating the soil microbiota. Valorizing this immense volume of residue into biochar presents a compelling circular economy paradigm.

However, paddy straw biochar differs fundamentally from bamboo or hardwood biochar. While it retains a functional fixed carbon content, fast-growing agricultural residues inherently possess a very high proportion of inorganic constituents drawn from the soil. The ash content of paddy straw biochar frequently ranges between 8.5% and 33.4%, heavily dependent on the specific soil chemistry of the harvest location and the pyrolysis conditions. This ash is predominantly composed of biogenic silica (SiO2) and alkaline metal oxides, specifically potassium oxide (K2O) and sodium oxide (Na2O). The heavy presence of silica and alkalis imposes complex metallurgical challenges regarding slag chemistry, melt viscosity, and absolute slag volume, which strictly limits the maximum safe blending ratio of paddy straw biochar in the SAF.

## 4. Electrical and Rheological Dynamics in the Submerged Arc Furnace
Integrating biochar into the HCFeCr Submerged Arc Furnace process is far more complex than simple volumetric substitution. It fundamentally alters the intricate electro-chemical ecosystem of the reactor. The technical feasibility of this transition hinges entirely on mastering three interdependent operational parameters: the electrical resistivity of the carbonaceous bed, the rheology and chemistry of the slag, and the specific reactivity of the reductant.

### 4.1 High-Temperature Electrical Resistivity
The electrical resistivity of the burden materials in a SAF is the paramount operational variable. It dictates the physical distribution of thermal energy. Optimal, moderately low resistivity ensures the deep penetration of the electrodes into the burden, stabilizing the electrical arc and maintaining the extreme temperatures required in the lower smelting zone.
Conventional metallurgical coke possesses relatively low electrical resistivity, establishing a highly conductive, stable pathway. Conversely, at ambient temperatures, biochar and charcoal act nearly as electrical insulators, exhibiting resistivity values orders of magnitude higher than coke. If uncalibrated, replacing coke with room-temperature biochar would drastically increase the overall resistance of the burden. This would force the automated electrodes to ride dangerously high in the furnace to maintain the set current, resulting in superheated off-gases, cold hearths, incomplete smelting, and severe structural damage to the furnace roof.

However, advanced high-temperature evaluations fundamentally reshape this understanding. Rigorous research utilizing four-point probe setups to evaluate the electrical properties of charcoal and biochar up to 1700°C demonstrates a profound, non-linear physical transformation. As the biocarbon is subjected to increasing heat treatment temperatures—simulating its slow descent through the increasingly hot zones of the SAF burden—its resistivity decreases precipitously. By the time the biochar reaches temperatures exceeding 1400°C, a zone critical for the actual reduction reactions, its structural matrix undergoes a graphitization-like ordering of the carbon atoms. At these elevated temperatures, the electrical resistivity of biochar drops to mirror the resistivity of fossil carbon materials.

The primary operational constraint, therefore, is managing the resistivity in the cooler, upper zones of the furnace. To mitigate this high initial resistivity, raw biochar must be carefully blended with conventional conductive coal or coke. Alternatively, it must be processed into highly densified bio-briquettes using conductive binders. Such agglomeration strategies are currently under active, advanced investigation by regional metallurgical research institutions to optimize the feed for industrial scale-up.

### 4.2 Slag Chemistry, Viscosity, and Liquidus Temperature
The production of HCFeCr generates massive volumes of slag. Depending on the ore grade, the process yields approximately 1.1 to 1.6 tonnes of slag per tonne of valuable alloy. The primary matrix of ferrochrome slag lies within the complex SiO2−Al2O3−MgO−CaO−CrOx system, typically comprising 27% to 35% silica, 15% to 29% alumina, and 23% to 33% magnesia, alongside residual unreduced chromium oxides. Slag viscosity is a critical performance metric; it directly governs the physical separation of dispersed metallic alloy droplets from the molten slag matrix, thereby dictating the ultimate chromium recovery rates of the facility.

The introduction of biochar, particularly agricultural variants like paddy straw biochar, injects a disruptive new variable into the carefully balanced slag chemistry. The high biogenic silica and alkaline content (Na2O, K2O) of paddy straw ash act as powerful fluxing agents. Advanced neural network computations and empirical thermodynamic phase diagrams constructed for these complex slag systems indicate that incremental additions of silica and sodium oxide initially depress the liquidus temperature (the deformation temperature) and lower the viscosity of the slag. While a slightly lower viscosity can enhance metal-slag separation kinetics, an excessive drop in the liquidus temperature narrows the safe operational window of the SAF and can lead to highly aggressive, rapid erosion of the furnace's internal refractory linings.

Furthermore, high ash inputs inherently increase the total absolute volume of slag generated. Because melting slag is highly endothermic, a larger slag volume directly and linearly inflates the specific electricity consumption (kWh/t) of the furnace. Bamboo biochar, characterized by its exceedingly low ash content, circumvents these rheological issues almost entirely, functioning nearly identically to low-ash specialized coke in maintaining the established slag equilibrium. Conversely, the utilization of paddy straw biochar must be strictly capped—likely between 10% and 20% of the total carbon mix—and carefully counterbalanced by adjusting basic flux additions, such as burnt lime (CaO), to stabilize the anorthite and mullite mineral phases within the melt.

## 5. Life-Cycle Assessment and Carbon Abatement Potential
The primary, overriding motivation for navigating the intense metallurgical complexities of biochar substitution is its profound capability to decarbonize the SAF process. The comprehensive life-cycle assessment of HCFeCr production reveals a total Global Warming Potential ranging from 3.3 to 10.3 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent per tonne of alloy.

### 5.1 Quantifying Scope 1 Emission Mitigation
In a standard HCFeCr SAF operation operating within India, the production of one single tonne of alloy requires the consumption of approximately 500 to 700 kilograms of fixed carbon. This carbon is typically sourced from roughly 600 to 800 kilograms of raw metallurgical coal, coke, or coke breeze. The stoichiometric oxidation of this fossil carbon translates to direct Scope 1 emissions of approximately 1.5 to 2.2 tonnes of carbon dioxide per tonne of HCFeCr.

By strategically substituting fossil coal with biochar, the metallurgical facility transitions from emitting ancient, geologically sequestered carbon to emitting biogenic carbon. According to established global greenhouse gas accounting frameworks, including the IPCC guidelines, as well as India's newly minted Green Steel Taxonomy (which classifies "green steel" as having an emission intensity below 2.2 tonnes of CO2e per tonne of finished steel), biogenic carbon dioxide emissions are considered net-neutral. This accounting acknowledges that the carbon released during the smelting process was only recently sequestered from the atmosphere during the rapid growth phase of the biomass, such as bamboo or paddy crops, via photosynthesis.

If a facility in Odisha successfully substitutes 30% of its reductant requirement with bamboo biochar, it effectively and permanently abates 0.45 to 0.66 tonnes of Scope 1 carbon dioxide per tonne of alloy. A complete 100% theoretical substitution would abate the entire 1.5 to 2.2 tonnes of Scope 1 emissions per tonne of FeCr, fundamentally transforming the carbon intensity of the final output and drastically lowering the overall life-cycle assessment metrics.

Calculating the projected cost impact—expressed standardly as the Marginal Abatement Cost in dollars per tonne of carbon dioxide saved ($/tCO2 saved)—requires an integrated assessment of capital expenditures, operational expenditures, volatile commodity pricing, and the potential monetization of carbon credits.

## 9. Conclusion
The substitution of metallurgical coal and coke with biochar in the production of High-Carbon Ferrochrome represents a technologically formidable, yet profoundly essential pathway toward decarbonizing heavy industry. In the context of Odisha, India, this transition aligns perfectly with the national climate imperatives laid out in the Ministry of Steel's comprehensive "Greening the Steel Sector" roadmap. Bamboo biochar offers a near-seamless, high-performance substitute. Its exceptionally low ash content, high fixed carbon yield, and highly favorable high-temperature electrical resistivity characteristics make it highly compatible with the rigorous, extreme demands of Submerged Arc Furnaces.

With a projected marginal abatement cost ranging from $29 to $45 per tonne of carbon dioxide saved, the standalone economics are currently challenging for operators. However, the operationalization of India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, combined with the looming threat of international carbon tariffs such as CBAM, provides the necessary financial architecture to monetize these emission reductions, converting environmental compliance into a strategic, long-term economic advantage.
